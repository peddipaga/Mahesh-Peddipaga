Part 2 · The Building Blocks

Level-setting: a share, and what an option adds

Foundation

A share of stock

A small piece of ownership in a real business. If you own one share of a company, you own a sliver of its factories, its brand, its future profits. There is no expiry date. You may hold it for forty years.

You make money if the business becomes more valuable, or pays you a dividend. You lose if it becomes less valuable. The most you can lose is what you paid.

What we add today

An option

Not ownership. A contract — an agreement between two people about a share, at a fixed price, until a fixed date. Then it ceases to exist.

  • It is created when two parties agree, not issued by the company
  • It has an expiry date — this changes everything
  • It is a side agreement; the company knows nothing about it

Keep this picture: a share is a field you own. An option is a written agreement about that field that expires on a certain date. Owning the field and holding a paper about the field are two very different things.

The field/paper analogy carries through the whole deck. Repeat it later at the covered-call slide.
Part 2 · The Building Blocks

What is an option, really?

An option is the right — but not the obligation — to buy or sell something, at a price you fix today, until a date you fix today. You pay for that right.

The advance-on-land analogy

Suppose a piece of land is worth $300,000 today. You believe a new highway will be announced in three months. You pay the owner $6,000 for a written agreement: "For the next three months, I may buy this land for $300,000 if I choose. If I do not choose, you keep the $6,000."

  • Highway announced, land now $390,000 — you exercise and buy at $300,000. You gained $90,000, minus the $6,000 you paid.
  • No highway, land still $300,000 — you walk away. You lost exactly $6,000, not one dollar more.
  • The owner kept $6,000 for agreeing to wait. That was his income, and his risk.

That is a call option

The right to BUY at a fixed price.

Reverse it and you have a put

You pay the buyer for the right to SELL your land to him at $300,000, whatever happens. That is insurance against the price falling.

Both sides are always present

Every option has a buyer who paid, and a seller (a "writer") who received the money and took on an obligation. Your gain is exactly his loss.

Ask: "Has anyone here put earnest money down on a house, or paid a token advance on land?" — most hands go up, and the concept is already theirs. If your group thinks in rupees for property, say the same numbers as ₹50 lakh land with a ₹1 lakh advance; the arithmetic is identical.
Part 2 · The Building Blocks

Five words you must know

1 · Underlying

The thing the contract is about.

The stock, the index, the ETF. If you would not want to own the underlying itself, ask yourself why you want a contract about it.

2 · Strike price

The fixed price in the agreement.

The price at which you may buy (call) or sell (put). It never changes for the life of the contract. ₹50 lakh in our land example.

3 · Expiration

The date the agreement dies.

Weekly, monthly, or longer. After it passes, the contract is worth exactly zero, forever. This is the single most under-estimated fact by beginners.

4 · Premium

The price of the contract itself.

What the buyer pays and the seller receives. Quoted per share — so a premium of $2.00 costs $200, because one contract covers 100 shares.

5 · Contract size

One contract = 100 shares (US).

Multiply everything by 100. This is where beginners get hurt: "it's only $3" is actually $300, and ten contracts is $3,000. In India, F&O contracts use exchange-set lot sizes instead — check the lot size before you do any arithmetic.

Hammer the ×100 point. Ask the room: "A $3.50 premium — what does one contract cost?" Wait for $350.

Pay a small, known amount. Lose often, but each loss is capped. They need a big move, soon.

If someone says "selling options wins 80% of the time" — agree, then ask what happens in the other 20%. That single question protects people.
Part 2 · The Building Blocks

Moneyness: is the contract useful right now?

In the money (ITM)

Exercising right now would give you something.
Call: stock is above the strike.
Put: stock is below the strike.

Expensive. Behaves most like the stock.

At the money (ATM)

Stock price ≈ strike price.

Maximum uncertainty, so the largest amount of "time value" — and therefore the fastest decay.

Out of the money (OTM)

Exercising now would be pointless.
Call: stock is below the strike.
Put: stock is above the strike.

Cheap — and cheap for a reason. Most expire worthless.

Where beginners are drawn — and lost

Far out-of-the-money options are the cheapest on the screen, so they look like the best value. They are the equivalent of a lottery ticket: a large payoff, multiplied by a small probability. The screen shows you the payoff. It does not show you the probability. "Cheap" is a price, not a bargain.

"Cheap is a price, not a bargain" — repeat it. This is the single sentence that stops the most damage among beginners.
Part 2 · The Building Blocks

Why time is the buyer's enemy

Premium = intrinsic value + time value

Intrinsic — what it is worth if exercised today. Real, solid.
Time value — what you pay for the possibility of a better price before expiry. This part melts away, every single day, and melts fastest in the final weeks.

The melting ice analogy

You bought a block of ice and you are carrying it across the courtyard. Every minute it is smaller. To profit, the price must move far enough, and fast enough, to outrun the melting. Being right about the direction but slow is still a loss.

The mirror image: for the option seller, this melting is income. That is why selling feels so pleasant — and why it lulls people to sleep.

Time value of a $100-strike call, stock at $100

Illustrative, Black–Scholes, 40% volatility. Notice the curve is not a straight line. Almost 40% of the option's original 90-day value is still lost in the final two weeks alone. A 90-day option decays gently; a 7-day option bleeds.

Point at the steep right end of the curve. Say: "Weekly options are almost pure melting ice — that is why they are the most popular and the most destructive."

Implied volatility — the price of uncertainty

Implied volatility (IV) is the market's collective guess about how much the stock will move — already baked into the premium you pay. It is not a prediction of direction, only of size of movement.

The umbrella-price analogy

Umbrellas are cheap in the dry season and expensive when black clouds are gathering. Options work the same way. Before a company's results announcement, everyone expects a storm — so every contract is priced high. You can buy an umbrella at the worst possible price and still get rained on.

Two ways to be right and still lose

  • You bought expensive uncertainty. The stock moved the way you predicted — but less than the price already assumed. You lose.
  • The storm passed. After the announcement, uncertainty collapses and every option instantly loses value. This is called IV crush, and we will see it with real numbers shortly.

Practical rule for beginners: if you cannot explain whether the option you are buying is expensive or cheap relative to its own history, you do not yet know what you are paying for.

Spreads — putting a fence around the loss

"For which of you, desiring to build a tower, does not first sit down and count the cost, whether he has enough to complete it?"
మీలో ఎవడైనను ఒక గోపురముకట్టింపగోరినయెడల దానిని కొనసాగించుటకు కావలసినది తన యొద్ద ఉన్నదో లేదో అని కూర్చుండి తగులుబడి మొదట లెక్కచూచుకొనడా?
Luke 14:28 · లూకా 14:28
In context Jesus is speaking about the cost of discipleship, not project budgeting — so we borrow it as an analogy, honestly labelled. But the instinct He appeals to is exactly right here: know the full cost before you begin, not after.

What a spread is

You buy one option and sell another on the same stock at the same time — different strike, usually the same expiry. The one you sell pays for part of the one you buy.

  • Cheaper to enter than a bare option
  • Both the maximum gain and the maximum loss are known before you enter — printed on the screen
  • Less damage from time decay and from overpaying for volatility

What you give up

The unlimited upside. You have sold away the far-away outcome — which, honestly, was never likely and was mostly what made the position feel exciting.

A spread does not make a bad idea good. It only ensures that a bad idea has a known, survivable price. That is not a small thing — it is most of what risk management is.

Part 5 · Straddles & Strangles

Betting on movement rather than direction

Sometimes you are confident that something big will happen — a results announcement, a court ruling, an election, a drug trial — but you have no idea which way it will go. Buying a call and a put together lets you profit from a large move in either direction.

Straddle

Buy a call and a put at the same strike, same expiry — usually right at the current price.

Expensive, because you are buying two at-the-money contracts, each carrying maximum time value.

Strangle

Buy a call and a put at different strikes — the call above the price, the put below it.

Cheaper, because both are out of the money. But the move must be bigger before either one starts to pay.

Before we look at either — the uncomfortable truth

These are the strategies most often sold to beginners as "you win whichever way it moves." That is technically true and practically misleading. You are not competing against the stock's direction; you are competing against the price the market already charged you for expecting the movement. If everyone knows results are on Thursday, Thursday is already in the price.

Set this expectation before showing the charts, so the pretty V-shape doesn't do the persuading for you.

IV crush — right about the move, wrong about the money

A company announces results tomorrow. Uncertainty is high, so option prices are inflated. You buy the $100 straddle for $751 with 9 days left. The results come out. The uncertainty vanishes overnight — implied volatility falls from 60% to 30% — and every option loses value the instant the news is known.

Stock after resultsMoveStraddle now worthYour P/L
$1000%$354−$397
$103+3%$441−$310
$105+5%$569−$182
$107+7%$733−$18
$110+10%$1,014+$263

Black–Scholes, 8 days remaining, 4% rate. A 5% overnight jump — a genuinely big move — still loses you $182.

Why this is not a rare accident

The high price you paid was the market's forecast of the move. To profit, the actual move must exceed the forecast — and the forecast is made by thousands of well-resourced participants who also know the results date.

What the research finds

A study of 32,791 earnings announcements (2010–2021) by researchers at MIT Sloan and Stanford found retail option buyers around earnings lost 5–9% on typical announcements and 10–14% on high-volatility ones, with bid-ask spreads alone consuming close to 9–10% of the investment.

de Silva, Smith & So, "Losing is Optional: Retail Option Trading and Earnings Announcement Volatility."

This is the single most useful slide for anyone who has already been buying options around earnings. Give it time.

Why the odds are structured this way

1 · Zero-sum, minus costs. Options create no earnings. Every dollar won is a dollar lost by someone — and the broker, exchange and market maker are paid first, from both sides.

2 · Your opponent is not a person. It is a firm with better data, faster machines, lower costs and no emotions. You are not competing with your neighbour.

3 · No easy side. Time is against the buyer; the tails are against the seller. There is no corner of this market that is quietly easy.

What your broker is legally required to tell you

"An option holder who neither sells his option in the secondary market nor exercises it prior to its expiration will necessarily lose his entire investment in the option."

Options Clearing Corporation, Characteristics and Risks of Standardized Options, June 2024 — the disclosure booklet every US broker must give every options customer

Give the three dollar figures without drama — they are strong enough on their own. The middle card is the one people have never considered: the invisible spread cost was seven times the visible commission. Then read the OCC line aloud; it is the regulator's own words, not yours, and it cannot be argued with.

Two numbers you will hear — both wrong

✗ "93% of options traders lose money."
This is India's regulator (SEBI), studying Indian equity F&O, FY22–FY24. It circulates widely on US social media and WhatsApp with the country label removed. No US regulator has ever published such a figure — not the SEC, not FINRA.

✗ "90% of options expire worthless."
Repeated everywhere, always "according to CBOE," never with a source. The best-documented estimate is closer to 30% — most contracts are closed out before expiry, not held to zero.

Why correcting these matters

Both false numbers point the right direction, so it is tempting to use them. Don't. If one person in the room knows the "93%" is Indian data, everything true you said afterwards becomes suspect.

The verified US case is strong enough: the costs are large, certain and paid up front; the average return is negative; and the drain is slow enough to feel like skill for years.

If you proceed: size is the whole safety system

The three ceilings

Total options allocation≤ 5% of investable assets
Any single position≤ 1% of investable assets
Assume every buy goes tozero

Worked example

A family with $50,000 in investments: at most $2,500 total in options, and at most $500 in any one trade. That is two or three contracts of the examples we saw today. It will feel too small to bother with.

That feeling is the point. A position that cannot hurt your family also cannot panic you into a worse decision at 2am.

Why "just this once, a bit bigger" is the trap

Losses do not undo symmetrically. Lose 50% and you need +100% to return to even. Lose 80% and you need +400%. This asymmetry is why a single oversized position can end a decade of careful saving — and why the discipline must be set before the position, never during it.

Before entering, write four numbers down

  1. Why — the specific reason, in one sentence a child could follow
  2. Maximum loss — the exact dollar figure, accepted in advance
  3. Exit if wrong — the price or date at which you close, decided now
  4. Exit if right — the profit at which you take it, decided now

If you cannot write all four in two minutes, that is not a trade. It is a hope with a ticker symbol.

The 50%→100% asymmetry surprises almost everyone. Write it on the board.

  1. Am I hoping to escape work, or to steward what work has already given me?
  2. Would I be at peace if this went to zero tomorrow morning?
  3. Does my spouse know the exact amount, today, without being asked?

  1. Have I ever increased a position to recover a loss?

Where we land

What we learned

  • An option is a right, not an obligation, with an expiry date — and expiry changes everything
  • Calls profit from a rise, puts from a fall; buyers have capped losses, sellers do not
  • Spreads fence the loss in advance; straddles and strangles bet on size of movement, not direction
  • Being right about direction is not enough — you must be right about size, timing, and price paid
  • The most defensible use is protection, not profit

And the honest bottom line: for most families most of the time, the right answer is patient, boring, diversified ownership — and a good night's sleep.

If you take nothing else from today, take two things: the eight house rules, and the order of operations — give, clear debt, build the emergency fund, insure, invest steadily. Options come last, if at all.

Sensible next steps

  • Paper-trade for six months first. Record every trade and your reason.
  • Read the Characteristics and Risks of Standardized Options booklet your broker is required to give you. Most people never open it.
  • Check the tax treatment before, not after. In the US, options gains are generally short-term capital gains taxed at ordinary income rates.
  • Talk to a fee-only, fiduciary advisor — paid by you, not by commission.
